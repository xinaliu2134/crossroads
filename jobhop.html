How to decide: quit or stay

Most people asking already feel the answer — they just can’t tell whether they’re reacting to a bad week or a real dead end. The decisive test is whether the problem is structural (built into the job and unchangeable) or situational (tied to a moment that will pass or could be fixed).

Structural problems — no growth path, a values mismatch, stable toxicity, far-below-market pay with no route to a raise — are reasons to leave. Situational ones — one brutal project, a manager who may move on, fixable burnout — are reasons to fix it in place first.

  • The disappearance test — if your biggest complaint vanished tomorrow, would you happily stay two more years? Yes = situational.
  • Growth — is there still a real path to learn and advance, or have you plateaued?
  • Money — underpaid vs. market is a strong reason, but try negotiating before quitting.
  • Health — a genuinely toxic environment affecting your wellbeing overrides the “line up the next job first” rule.
  • Timing — if you can, secure the next role first and leave with something to show for your time.

Common questions

How do I know if I should quit my job?

Quit when the problem is structural — no growth, a values mismatch, stable toxicity, or far-below-market pay with no fix. Stay or fix it in place when the cause is recent, situational, or tied to one project or manager that may change.

Should I quit without another job lined up?

Usually not, unless your health is at risk or you have substantial savings. It’s easier to find work while employed, and a gap can weaken your leverage.

How long should I stay at a job before leaving?

There’s no fixed rule, but staying long enough to deliver something meaningful — often a year or more — protects your story and usually means you’ve gotten the learning available.
