How to decide: grad school or work

Grad school is worth it when it clearly moves you toward a specific goal — and risky when it’s a way to delay deciding what you want. Going back to school just because you’re unsure what to do next is usually an expensive way to postpone the question.

Weigh the real return, not the prestige. Combine what the degree costs (tuition plus the income you give up) against the earnings and opportunities it actually unlocks in your field.

  • Does your target job require it? If the role strongly prefers or mandates the degree, that’s the strongest reason to go.
  • ROI — tuition + forgone salary vs. the realistic earnings bump in your specific industry.
  • Work experience first — most successful students worked in the field first; it sharpens what you get from the program.
  • Motivation — a concrete goal beats “I’m not sure what else to do.”
  • Program outcomes — strong programs publish placement rates and salaries; hidden numbers are a red flag.

Common questions

Is grad school worth it?

It’s worth it when it clearly moves you toward a goal your target career actually requires or rewards. It’s riskier when the payoff is vague or you’re mainly trying to avoid deciding what you want.

Should I work before grad school?

Usually yes. Working first builds savings, clarifies whether you even need the degree, and helps you get far more out of the program if you do enroll.

How do I calculate the ROI of a master’s degree?

Add tuition plus the salary you’d give up while studying, then compare that to the realistic increase in pay and opportunities the degree unlocks in your field — not the national average.
